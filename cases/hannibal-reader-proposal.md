# Hannibal: the person and the accounts that remain

Reader-page proposal, 18 September 2026. Not in the public catalogue.
Based on candidate revision `86d22fd575c0d3da51a7b866484501796e523464`.

## What reaches us

A name can be familiar while the evidence behind it is less familiar. Here we
follow a few passages about Hannibal, rather than try to fit a whole life onto
one page. The question is simple: when we read about him now, whose account are
we reading?

Polybius describes Hannibal's descent from the Alps into Italy. He also tells
readers how he obtained some of his information. In one passage, he says he
spoke with people who had been present and crossed the Alps himself to examine
the country. In another, he says he used troop lists on a bronze tablet at the
Lacinian promontory. These are claims Polybius makes about his sources and his
work. Our evidence here is his surviving account as presented on a modern
reading page, not the interviews or the tablet themselves.

[Read Polybius, Book 3](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/3*.html):
3.48.12 for his account of inquiry and inspection; 3.33.17–18 for the tablet;
3.56.3–4 for the descent and troop figures.

## A source we can read names sources we have not read

Cornelius Nepos reports that Silenus and Sosylus lived with Hannibal in camp
and wrote about the war. He also identifies Sosylus as Hannibal's teacher of
Greek literature. This gives us a report about people close to Hannibal. It
does not give this record their own accounts: those works have not been
inspected here.

[Read Nepos, chapter 13, paragraph 3](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/nepos-hannibal/chapter-13).
The page separates the Latin text from modern commentary.

Polybius, meanwhile, sharply criticises Sosylus and Chaereas in 3.20.5.
That criticism belongs to Polybius. Recording it is different from adopting
his judgment. Counting these two ancient authors would not establish two
independent witnesses to the same event: the passages make different claims.

## What this small record leaves open

The reading path here is through Greek and Roman accounts. It does not offer
a balanced collection of Carthaginian testimony or a complete account of how
the surviving writers depended on earlier material.

Even a physical object needs care. The translation describes a bronze tablet
in 3.33 and an inscription on a column at Lacinium in 3.56. This record has not
established whether those descriptions refer to the same physical object.

The Polybius website presents a 1922 English translation and warns that its
page has not yet been proofread. We have checked selected passages on that
page, not manuscripts or a critical edition. We have not investigated the
exact Alpine pass, Hannibal's appearance, or all surviving fragments of works
about him. Unexamined evidence is not evidence that nothing survives.

## Why keep this encounter?

Preserving an account of a person involves more than preserving their name.
It also means keeping the route back to particular evidence visible, including
the point where that route stops. A later reader should be able to distinguish
what an author reports, what this record checked, and what remains open.

Hannibal was chosen to test this problem, not as a representative person or
as a judgment about whose life matters most. “Hannibal Barca” is the candidate's
conventional modern label; the passages checked here attest “Hannibal”.
